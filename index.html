Demos

Four browser-only demos by Ian Mauck. Nothing is uploaded, no account is created on a server, and every portfolio, fund, manager and letter is synthetic; the few published figures are cited where they appear.

Demo 01

Allocate

A close-the-month tool for allocators: what can become cash and by when, where the portfolio sits against policy, what changed at the managers, and a review with a memo.

Open Allocate ›
In one sentence
A monthly tool for people running a pool of money, like a university's, across many funds: what can become cash, whether the split matches its plan, and whether fund managers stay within their stated limits.
Who it is for
The team investing for a university, a charity or a wealthy family across many outside funds.
The situation
Every month they ask: how fast could we raise cash, when some funds pay out slowly? Is the money still split the way the board decided, as some investments outgrow others? Are the fund managers doing what they said?
What you do here
Press Try the demo, open Portfolio 1 (made-up data), and follow five steps: Data, Liquidity (cash), Policy (the planned split), Managers and Review.
What to look for
The Liquidity step. It sorts the money by how fast it could become cash, from a day to years, and shows when funds you promised money to will next ask for it.
What it assumes
The example holdings, including every fund, value, cash movement and document, are made up. The market averages it compares against are rough stand-ins, not official figures, which cost money to license. Private funds report only every three months, and the tool never guesses a newer value, so their values are often about three months old. Nothing leaves your browser.
Demo 02

Manager Monitor

Reads a fund manager's quarterly letter, extracts what matters, cites the sentence behind every value, and drafts the change memo. Scripted mode, no calls to any AI service.

Open Manager Monitor ›
In one sentence
A tool that reads the letter a fund manager sends every three months, pulls out key facts, shows the sentence behind each one, and drafts a short note on what changed since the last letter.
Who it is for
Someone at an investment office who reads each firm's letter every three months and writes up what changed.
The situation
Each letter covers the same facts: how much money the firm manages, its team, fees, results and anything unusual. Pulling them out, spotting what changed and writing a memo is slow, and easy to get wrong.
What you do here
Pick a firm and a quarter. The tool lists the facts, ranks what changed by importance and drafts the memo. Here the letters are made up and the results saved, not read live by AI.
What to look for
The citation. Click any number and the letter scrolls to its source sentence; a number without one is not worth trusting.
What it assumes
The 17 managers and their letters are made up for the demo. The public demo shows answers saved in advance instead of asking an AI live, so no sign-up is needed and it gives the same answer every time. A live mode exists but is turned off here.
Demo 03

Fund Sim

A seed fund portfolio construction simulator: set the checks, the price, the reserves and the follow-on rule, and it runs thousands of funds on power-law outcomes to show the spread of net multiples, when cash comes back, and what reserves a strategy actually needs. Illustrative assumptions, not market data.

Open Fund Sim ›
In one sentence
A simulator that runs thousands of imaginary startup-investing funds to show how choices, like how many startups to fund and how much to save for extra bets on the ones doing well, change the results.
Who it is for
Anyone curious how a venture capital fund (a fund that invests in startups) decides how many to back.
The situation
A venture fund puts money into many startups knowing most will fail and one or two might pay for everything. Before picking any, it decides how much to put into each and how much to keep for investing more in the ones that do well.
What you do here
Change those choices and the simulator runs 10,000 made-up funds with the same rules. Every starting number is an example, not market data.
What to look for
How widely results swing: how often the fund avoids a loss, and how often one startup alone pays back the whole fund.
What it assumes
Every starting number is a labeled example you can change, not market data. Results follow a power law: most startups fail and a few big wins earn most of the money. The random draws come from a fixed seed, a set number that makes any run repeatable. An in-app panel compares results with published figures and lists the model's shortcuts.
Demo 04

Runway

Will the money arrive before the cash runs out? A cash-runway model for a young software company selling to institutions. Illustrative assumptions, not market data.

Open Runway ›
In one sentence
A tool that shows how long a young software company can keep going while it waits for universities, charities and investment firms to start paying for what it sells.
Who it is for
A founder, an early employee or an investor in small companies that sell software to organizations investing large sums, like a university's investment office.
The situation
In this model, a committee has to approve each purchase, and the money comes out of a yearly budget that is set months before the year begins. A customer who says yes in the spring may not pay until the next budget year starts, while the company pays salaries every month.
What you do here
Set the company's starting cash and monthly spending, how many customers it wins each month and what each pays. Then set the buyer's calendar: when the budget year starts, how often the committee meets and how far ahead the budget is fixed. Watch three cash lines: the founder's plan, the buyer's calendar, and the company with no customers at all. You can also save versions side by side, or look at five made-up companies at once.
What to look for
How much later the first customer's money arrives on the buyer's calendar, whether cash drops below zero before then, and how much more cash the calendar needs than the founder's plan.
What it assumes
Both lines win the same customers at the same price. Only the timing differs. Each customer pays a year at a time, a month after each bill, and by default nine in ten renew each year. Spending stays flat. The calendar delays deals but never loses them, and no buyer pays mid-year from a spare budget. All the numbers are made up for illustration.

All portfolios, funds, managers and letters here are synthetic, every fund parameter in Fund Sim is an illustrative assumption, every company, prospect and figure in Runway is made up, and nothing describes a real institution, fund, manager, company or client. Nothing here is investment advice.
